GT:  Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy 
GT Discussion:  Background Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) remains a significant cause of neonatal mortality worldwide. Despite advancements, up to 23% of affected neonates die, and 25% experience permanent neurological deficits [1]. While clinical criteria can guide diagnosis, MRI is essential for definitive evaluation, especially in cases of atypical presentation or discordant clinical findings. MRI can identify specific patterns of injury, assess disease progression, and predict long-term outcomes. Clinical Perspective Preterm neonates are more vulnerable to HIE than full-term infants. Brain damage can occur within a few minutes of a hypoxic-ischemic event. Clinical diagnosis is based on evidence of foetal distress, proven acidosis, low Apgar score, need for resuscitation, neurological signs of encephalopathy, and multiorgan dysfunction, without evidence of infection, congenital malformations, or metabolic disorders. Imaging Perspective MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing HIE, with findings differing between preterm and full-term neonates, given variations in the arterial supply. The injury pattern is influenced by brain maturity and the severity of hypoperfusion. In the acute phase, DWI shows restricted diffusion, that typically pseudo-normalises by the end of the first week [2]. In term newborns with profound acute hypoxia, the “basal ganglia-thalamus pattern” is predominant, correlating with motor impairment. T1 hyperintensity of grey matter begins on the 2nd day of life, and persists for months, while T2WI hyperintensity (oedema) appears by the 3rd day. Chronic HIE is characterised by atrophy and T2 hyperintensity. In contrast, full-term infants suffering prolonged partial or mild-to-moderate asphyxia exhibit a watershed pattern affecting the parieto-occipital and posterior temporal lobes, leading to atrophy known as ulegyria [3]. Spectroscopy MRI (SRM) in the basal ganglia can reveal decreased N-acetylaspartate (NAA) and increased lactate levels, with increased lactate-to-creatinine and lactate-to-NAA ratios indicating a worse neurological outcome. MRI also helps rule out other causes of encephalopathy, including haemorrhage, perinatal stroke, neoplasms, and congenital malformations. Outcome The pattern of lesions, particularly the involvement of basal ganglia, cortical lesions, abnormal DWI, and SRM findings, is associated with poor prognosis and persistent neurological impairment. Marked EEG abnormalities are also linked to a worse prognosis. While mild HIE in term infants often resolves, 25% develop significant neurologic sequelae. Early identification and prompt management in paediatric intensive care units are crucial for optimal outcomes [2]. Take Home Message Neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is a clinical-radiological spectrum of brain injury following perinatal ischemia. Early diagnosis is crucial for optimal management and prognosis. MRI is essential for diagnosis, assessing injury severity, and predicting outcomes. GT Discussion:  Rapidly growing sternal and parasternal masses are rare in the paediatric population but can be concerning due to the potential suspicion of malignancy [1]. In 2010, Winkel et al. described SELSTOC (self-limiting sternal tumour of childhood) as asymptomatic, self-limiting, and typically self-involuting tumours, that arise from an exaggerated immune response to an aseptic inflammatory process, with no history of trauma. The aetiology of these acute sternal lesions of non-neoplastic and non-infectious origin remains uncertain [2,3]. Clinically, SELSTOC presents as a firm mass, generally 1–4.5 cm in diameter, located in presternal or parasternal regions. Occasionally, there may be red or blue discolouration, local tenderness, or mild fever. Laboratory findings are typically unremarkable, though mild elevations in C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) may occur [1]. Radiologically, SELSTOC shows no aggressive features. Ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality and often reveals a characteristic “dumbbell-shape”, with presternal and retrosternal components, and connected by a narrow neck between the segments of the sternum or between the sternum and the adjacent rib cartilage. These lesions appear well-defined, hypoechoic relative to surrounding subcutaneous fat, and may be bordered by reactive fluid. They show no internal vascularisation and demonstrate no invasion of adjacent bone or muscle, nor any connection to the skin. In contrast to malignant sternal masses, which exhibit invasive growth and destruction of adjacent tissues, SELSTOC remains localised and gradually diminishes [2,4]. Diagnosis is established by observing spontaneous reduction in size over a few weeks or until complete resolution, generally within an 18-month period. So, ultrasound follow-up is recommended to monitor for shrinkage [1,3]. CT and MRI do not play a significant role in the diagnosis of this entity. On MRI, restricted diffusion has been reported in some cases, so this finding should not be misinterpreted as indicative of malignancy or abscess [1,4]. The differential diagnoses include malignant sternal lesions, which typically exhibit aggressive signs of destruction, invasion of neighbouring structures, and a tendency to increase in size, unlike SELSTOC. Osteomyelitis is another consideration; however, SELSTOC does not align clinically or radiologically. Notably, it lacks periosteal reaction, cortical erosion or destruction, increased fatty marrow density, and other signs consistent with osteomyelitis [3,4] . The absence of aggressive behaviour and the characteristic appearance on ultrasound support the “wait-and-see” approach, which was implemented in this case . On a follow-up ultrasound examination conducted two weeks later, a reduction of the lesion size was observed, and demonstrated complete resolution at the six-month follow-up. In summary, SELSTOC should be suspected in infants and children presenting with a rapidly growing, sternum-adjacent mass, particularly when accompanied by characteristic ultrasound findings, lack of systemic symptoms, and an otherwise normal physical examination. This profile supports a conservative “wait-and-see” approach, as SELSTOC represents a “do not touch” lesion in paediatric practice [1,2]. GT:  Duodenum inversum 
GT Discussion:  Duodenum inversum, also known as inverted duodenum or duodenum reflexum, is an uncommon congenital anatomical variation that alters the path of the duodenum. In this variant anatomy , the third part of the duodenum folds backwards over the second part and crosses the midline higher up than usual. However, the duodenojejunal junction stays in its usual anatomical position [1,2]. Typical results from an upper gastrointestinal series show contrast flowing from the second part of the duodenum back into the first part and then into the bulb, along with a delay in movement through the duodenum, and a quick passage of contrast through the third part of the duodenum [2]. This is unlike the redundant duodenum, where the descending duodenum is longer and redundant; however, it crosses the midline at the expected level. The duodenum inversum is believed to occur because the dorsal mesentery persists, allowing the duodenum to remain mobile. Most individuals with this condition do not show symptoms or experience vague symptoms like epigastric pain, nausea, or bloating [3]. Being familiar with this variant anatomy is important so that it is not mistaken as malrotation, which can lead to unnecessary surgical intervention. GT Discussion:  Hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSPs) are a heterogeneous group of inherited neurodegenerative disorders characterised by progressive spasticity and weakness of the lower limbs. They are broadly classified as “pure” (spastic paraparesis alone) and “complicated” forms (associated with additional neurological features such as cognitive decline, epilepsy, or ataxia) [4]. MRI plays a pivotal role in diagnosis, especially when clinical features are nonspecific. Atrophy of the corpus callosum is a well-recognised imaging marker in complicated HSP syndromes, particularly SPG11 and SPG15 [1]. The “ear of the lynx” sign, seen as bilateral periventricular frontal white matter hyperintensities, is considered highly suggestive of SPG11-associated HSP [2,5]. Spinal cord atrophy is another supportive imaging feature, reflecting long-tract degeneration [3]. Early recognition of these imaging markers can direct genetic testing, shorten diagnostic delays, and aid family counselling. Differential diagnoses include leukodystrophies, demyelinating disorders and other causes of hereditary leukopathies. Take Home Message / Teaching Points Corpus callosum atrophy and the “ear of the lynx” sign are highly suggestive of complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia. Recognition of these features on MRI helps guide genetic testing and avoid unnecessary work-up. Spinal cord atrophy is a supportive imaging finding reflecting corticospinal tract degeneration.
